# Deep Frying

Cooking food fully submerged in hot oil, around 350°F, until it is crisp outside and cooked
through. Needed for [[killu-bonda]].

Note that this page is general cooking knowledge, not family instruction: [[killu-bonda]]'s source
gives ingredients only and no method at all, so none of the detail below is attributable to it.

## Why it works
Oil holds and transfers heat far better than air, and unlike water it can go well above 100°C. So
the surface of a fritter hits browning temperature almost immediately while the inside is still
only steaming.

The crust is built by water leaving. As the batter's surface heats, its water flashes to steam and
rushes outward, and that outward pressure is what keeps the oil from soaking in while the starch
sets and the proteins and sugars brown. Inside, the vegetables cook gently in their own steam at
not much over boiling point.

Oil temperature is therefore the entire game, and both directions fail for the same reason:

- **Too cool** and the steam pressure is too weak to hold the oil out. Oil moves in instead of
  water moving out, and the bonda comes out heavy and greasy rather than crisp.
- **Too hot** and the crust browns and sets before the heat has reached the middle, so you get a
  dark shell around raw batter.

This is also why crowding matters, and it is not about space. Every cold piece you add absorbs
heat from the oil, and the steam coming off it carries more away. Put in too many at once and the
oil drops into the greasy regime for the rest of the batch.

## Steps
1. Heat 2-3 inches of oil in a deep, heavy pan - a [[dutch-oven]] holds its temperature well when
  cold batter goes in. Fill it no more than a third; the level rises when food is added.
2. Bring it to about 350°F. A thermometer is the honest way.
3. Test with the batter itself: drop in a small blob. It should sink briefly, rise, bubble
  steadily and colour in about a minute. Sitting on the bottom means too cool; darkening within
  seconds means too hot.
4. Get the batter right before you start. It wants to be thick enough to hold its shape on the
  spoon, not pour. A thin batter sheds into the oil and leaves the filling bare.
5. Add the raising agent last, just before frying - the baking soda or eno in a bonda batter
  starts working immediately, and a rested batter has lost its lift.
6. Lower in a few pieces at a time, leaving room for them to move, and nudge them apart so they
  don't fuse.
7. Turn them as they colour so both faces get the same time.
8. Lift out and drain on a rack. Paper in a pile traps steam under the fritters and softens the
  crust you just made. Salt immediately if you are salting.
9. Let the oil climb back to temperature between batches.

## When it's done
Deep golden brown, and - more reliably - the bubbling slows noticeably, because the vigorous
bubbling is water leaving the food and it tails off as the crust dries. A finished bonda feels
light and hard-shelled when nudged with a spoon, and sounds faintly hollow. If it still feels
heavy, there is water left inside.

## Common mistakes
- **Oil below temperature.** The commonest fault and the one that produces greasy, oil-logged
  fritters, for the reason above. Always test before the first batch.
- **Oil too hot.** A dark crust and a raw middle, and no way to fix it after the fact.
- **Crowding the pan.** The temperature crashes, the pieces stick to each other, and the whole
  batch comes out soft.
- **A batter that pours.** It disperses in the oil instead of coating, and you lose the filling
  into the pan where it burns and taints the oil.
- **Overfilling the pan.** Oil rises sharply when wet batter goes in, and oil over the side of a
  hot pan is the one genuine danger here. Keep water well away from the pan.
- **Draining in a heap on paper.** Steam has nowhere to go and re-softens the base.
- **Not skimming between batches.** Loose scraps of batter burn, and burnt fragments make the
  next batch taste bitter.
