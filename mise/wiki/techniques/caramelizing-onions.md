# Caramelizing Onions

Cooking onions past translucent and on into brown, slowly, until they turn sweet. [[borscht]]
singles it out: the author calls it "the one different thing" about the recipe.

## Why it works
An onion is around 90% water, with a good amount of sugar and a set of sharp sulphur compounds
that give it its raw bite. Cooking it runs through three distinct stages, and most recipes only
use the first two.

First the heat ruptures the cell walls and the water starts leaving: the onion slumps, turns
translucent and tastes mildly sweet. This is softening, and it is not caramelising. While the
surface is still wet, nothing can brown, because a wet surface cannot rise much above the boiling
point of water.

Once enough water has gone, the surface temperature climbs and two browning reactions start
together: the sugars caramelise, and Maillard browning takes place between those sugars and the
onion's amino acids. Between them they produce the colour and the deep, roasted sweetness - none
of which is in the raw onion. Given time, the harsh sulphur compounds also break down, which is
why a slowly browned onion tastes sweet rather than pungent.

Low heat is what buys that time. On high heat the cut edges of each piece scorch before the
interiors have given up their water, and scorched onion is bitter, not sweet. That is the
difference from [[searing]], which deliberately browns a surface fast and leaves the inside
alone; here you want the whole piece transformed.

**The same cue, later in the pot.** [[borscht]] then has you stir in the [[tomato-paste]] and the
chopped tomato and cook until the mixture "leaves its oil". That is the identical signal one step
on: the paste has given up its water, the emulsion breaks, and the fat separates out and pools
visibly round the edges. It is the cue the Indian pages use for a bhuna masala, and it means the
same thing - the water is gone, so browning can begin.

## Steps
1. Slice the onion evenly with a chefs knife. Uneven pieces brown at different rates, and the
  thin ones burn.
2. Heat 2-4 tbsp oil in a wide, heavy pan. [[borscht]] uses a [[dutch-oven]] and puts the garlic
  and bay leaves in with the onion.
3. Keep it at medium-low. Stir every couple of minutes rather than constantly, scraping the brown
  film off the base back into the onions - that film is the flavour, and left to itself it burns.
4. Translucent and soft comes at 5-8 minutes. Keep going from there; this is where most recipes
  stop and borscht does not.
5. If the pan is drying out or catching, add a splash of water to lift the fond and carry on.
  Don't turn the heat up to hurry it.
6. In borscht, the tomato paste and chopped tomato go in at this point and cook on until the
  mixture leaves its oil.

## When it's done
Borscht asks for slightly brown, not the dark jam of a long-cooked onion confit. The pieces are
limp and glossy, golden-brown at the edges, and they smell sweet rather than sharp - about a
quarter of an hour, not the hour a deep onion jam needs. The base of the pan carries a brown film
that dissolves back into the onions when you stir. For the tomato paste stage, look for clear oil
separating at the edge of the mixture and a visible darkening from bright red to brick.

## Common mistakes
- **Heat too high.** The edges scorch and go bitter while the middles are still raw and pungent.
  Bitterness from burnt onion spreads through the entire pot and cannot be seasoned out.
- **A crowded pan.** Too many onions release more water than can evaporate, so they simply stew
  in it and stay pale however long you give them. Use a wider pan.
- **Stirring constantly.** Browning needs contact time with the hot pan. Stir enough to stop it
  catching, not enough to keep it moving.
- **Stopping at translucent and calling it done.** That is a softened onion, and the sweetness and
  the colour both arrive only afterwards.
- **Adding liquid or tomato too early.** The pan drops straight back to boiling point and all
  browning stops. The onions are as brown as they are ever going to get at that moment.
- **Deglazing a black base.** A brown film is flavour; a black, acrid one is not, and a pot built
  on it tastes burnt from the first spoonful. Start again.
