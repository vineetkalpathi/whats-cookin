# Folding in Egg Whites

Combining whipped egg whites into a heavy batter without knocking the air out of them. In
[[almond-flour-cake]] this is not a refinement, it is the whole leavening story.

## Why it works
Whipping unfolds the proteins in egg white; they re-form around the air you are beating in, as a
thin film that traps the bubbles. Beating sugar in near the end dissolves into the water between
those films, thickening it so it drains more slowly - which both stabilises the foam and makes it
much harder to over-whip. That is why [[almond-flour-cake]] holds back half its sugar for the
whites and puts the other half in the yolks.

In a flourless cake there is no gluten network and almost nothing else producing gas. Almond flour
is heavy, damp and fatty, and the teaspoon of baking powder is a long way from enough to lift it.
So the air you fold in and keep *is* the rise you get. Knock it out and the cake cannot recover,
because nothing in the batter will generate more - you get a dense, slightly damp almond slab
instead of a sponge.

**Why the first addition is sacrificed.** The base here is deliberately a thick dough: almond
flour, baking powder and salt stirred into the yolks. Tip all the whites onto something that stiff
and you have to work hard and long to get them through it, and that shearing is what tears the
bubble films. So the first 1/2 cup goes in without ceremony - you are not trying to preserve it,
you are using it to slacken the dough to roughly the consistency of the foam itself. Everything
after that combines with very little movement, and the air in those later additions survives.

## Steps
1. Whip the whites with an [[electric-mixer]] to soft peaks: a peak that holds for a moment and
  then flops over at the tip.
2. Slowly beat in the sugar - half the recipe's sugar - until the foam is glossy. Set it aside and
  use it promptly.
3. Have the base ready and waiting, and the greased cake pan ready too. Whipped whites start
  draining from the moment you stop beating.
4. Add the whites 1/2 cup at a time. The first addition can be worked in briskly; from the second
  onward, fold.
5. Fold with a strong, broad spatula - a flimsy one bends in a dough this thick and ends up
  stirring. Cut down through the middle, sweep along the bottom of the bowl, lift the batter over
  itself, and turn the bowl a quarter turn between strokes.
6. Incorporate each addition fully before adding the next. The source is specific about this, and
  it is what keeps you from having to over-mix at the end to chase out streaks.
7. Stop the moment the batter is smooth and fluffy, and get it into the pan and the oven at once.

## When it's done
Smooth, fluffy and one even colour: no white streaks, and no pockets of unmixed dough at the
bottom of the bowl, which is where they hide. The batter should mound softly off the spatula and
hold its shape briefly rather than run off in a stream. If it has gone slack and pourable, the
foam has been beaten out of it.

## Common mistakes
- **Any fat in the whites.** A trace of yolk or a greasy bowl disrupts the protein film and the
  whites will not build volume. Separate carefully and start with a clean, dry bowl.
- **Whipping to stiff, dry peaks.** Over-whipped whites look grainy and begin to curdle; the films
  have already broken, so the foam collapses and weeps water as soon as you fold it. Soft peaks
  fold in far better.
- **Adding all the whites at once.** You then have to mix hard to combine them, which is precisely
  what destroys the air, and the thick dough guarantees it.
- **Stirring or beating instead of folding.** Stirring shears the bubble films; folding moves the
  batter over itself with far fewer strokes.
- **Under-folding.** Streaks of unincorporated white bake into rubbery pale patches and holes, and
  the cake rises unevenly.
- **Letting the whipped whites stand.** They drain and lose volume while they wait, so whip them
  only when the base is mixed and the pan is greased.
