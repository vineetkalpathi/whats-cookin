# Frying Curry Paste

Frying curry paste in hot fat before any liquid goes into the pot. Both [[thai-curry]] and
[[thai-coconut-butternut-squash-soup]] do it, and the soup pins it at exactly 3 minutes.

## Why it works
Curry paste out of the jar is raw. It is a grind of fibrous aromatics - chilli, lemongrass,
galangal, garlic, shallot, spices - and the ground state does nothing to cook them. Frying it does
three separate jobs.

- **It moves the flavour into the fat.** Most of the aroma compounds in those aromatics are
  fat-soluble, so hot oil pulls them out of the fibre and holds them, and the fat then carries them
  evenly through a litre of coconut milk. This is the same mechanism as [[blooming-spices]].
- **It gets the pan above boiling point.** The paste is wet. Until that water has cooked off, the
  pan is stuck at around 100°C and nothing browns; once it is dry, the paste's own sugars and amino
  acids brown and build the deeper, rounder notes that a raw paste has none of.
- **It drives off the raw edge.** The grassy, harsh taste of uncooked lemongrass, galangal and
  garlic cooks away in a couple of minutes and does not cook away later.

The timing is forced by what comes next. The moment the coconut milk or the broth goes in, the pan
drops to a simmer and browning stops for good - so whatever you have not developed in those few
minutes, you will not get. The slow cooker route in the squash soup makes the point by
omission: the paste goes in raw, with no oil, and never gets this stage at all. That is the price
of leaving the pot alone for eight hours.

## Steps
1. Fry the aromatics first. Both recipes do: the soup softens garlic and onion for about 3 minutes
  before the ginger and paste go in, and [[thai-curry]] works through green chilli, then garlic
  and ginger, then onion, then mint, in that order.
2. Add the paste to the hot fat over medium heat, and spread it over the base of the pan with the
  back of the spoon so as much of it as possible is in contact with the oil.
3. Keep it moving. The paste contains sugar and will catch if left to sit.
4. Give it about 3 minutes, which is what the soup specifies.
5. If it starts to stick before the time is up, add another spoonful of oil, not water. Water
  undoes the stage you are in the middle of.
6. Now add the liquid - broth, or coconut milk - and scrape the base clean so nothing burns under
  the simmer.
7. Home-ground paste, blitzed in a blender, comes out wetter than shop-bought and needs
  noticeably longer than 3 minutes to reach the same point.

## When it's done
Look for shine. [[thai-curry]] uses "cooked and shiny" as the cue for its aromatics, and the paste
gives you the same thing: it darkens a shade, the oil around it takes on its colour, and clear oil
begins to separate out at the edges, which is the water having left. The smell is the surer test -
it turns from sharp and raw to rounded and fragrant, and the change is obvious when it happens.
Three minutes at a steady medium is the benchmark; the shine is the actual answer.

## Common mistakes
- **Pouring the coconut milk in straight after the paste.** Nothing fries, nothing browns, and the
  finished curry tastes thin and raw in a way no amount of simmering fixes.
- **Too little fat.** With not enough oil the paste roasts on bare metal instead of frying in fat,
  and burnt chilli is bitter and unsalvageable.
- **Heat too high.** The sugar in the paste scorches fast. Medium, stirring, is right.
- **Leaving it alone.** Three minutes of unstirred paste in a thin pan is three minutes of it
  sticking to one spot.
- **Going by the clock only.** A wetter paste, a cooler pan or a bigger batch all need longer;
  separated oil and a changed smell are the real cues.
- **Skipping the aromatics.** The paste is a concentrate, not a whole flavour base; both recipes
  build fresh garlic, onion and ginger under it.
