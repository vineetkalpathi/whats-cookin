# Toasting Nuts

Browning nuts in a dry pan until they smell nutty and turn crisp. [[asian-cabbage-salad]] toasts
sliced [[almonds]] this way, is emphatic about not leaving the pan, and scatters them on at the
very end.

## Why it works
Nuts are roughly half fat and hold very little water, so they heat quickly and go almost straight
into Maillard browning - there is barely a drying stage to get through first. That browning builds
the toasted aroma, and the nut's own oil both carries it and releases it as it warms, which is why
a toasted nut smells of so much more than a raw one. The small amount of moisture inside also
cooks off, and that is what turns a nut that bends into one that snaps.

Two things explain why they burn so suddenly:

- **Their own oil conducts the heat inward** once the nut is hot, so the process accelerates
  rather than levelling off.
- **The browning happens on cut surfaces**, and sliced or flaked nuts are nearly all cut surface
  with almost no thickness behind it. A sliced almond goes from golden to black in seconds, where a
  whole one has a cool interior buying it time.

Carryover matters for the same reason. A pan that has just been taken off the heat is still well
above browning temperature, and nuts left sitting in it keep cooking.

In a salad there is a second reason to care. Crunch here is structural, and nuts take up moisture
from a dressing and go soft - which is exactly why [[asian-cabbage-salad]] adds them last rather
than tossing them through with everything else.

## Steps
1. Dry [[skillet]], medium heat, no oil. The nuts have plenty of their own.
2. Spread them in a single layer so they all touch the pan.
3. Shake the pan or stir more or less continuously. The source's instruction is to supervise the
  entire time and not walk away, and it is the one rule that matters.
4. Sliced almonds take about 3-5 minutes. Whole or halved nuts take longer and want slightly lower
  heat, so the inside dries before the outside is dark.
5. Tip them out of the pan and onto a cold plate the moment they are ready, not a second later.
6. Cool them completely. Warm nuts on a dressed salad soften straight away.
7. Scatter them over at the end, toss once, serve - the order [[asian-cabbage-salad]] uses.

## When it's done
Your nose tells you before your eyes do: the smell fills the kitchen, and that is the cue to look.
Sliced almonds want to be straw to light gold, with a few darker edges - judge by the pale cut
faces rather than the skins. Broken in half, a done nut snaps cleanly and smells sweet and nutty;
an underdone one bends and smells faintly dusty. Any sharp, acrid edge to the smell means they have
gone, and burnt nuts are bitter with nothing to be done about it.

## Common mistakes
- **Walking away.** The margin between golden and burnt is seconds, because the nut's own fat keeps
  driving the heat in. The source says so in as many words.
- **Leaving them in the pan after the heat is off.** The residual heat in a hot pan is enough to
  take them past done.
- **Heat too high.** The surface scorches before the inside has dried, so they taste burnt and
  still bend.
- **Two sizes in one pan.** Slices burn while whole nuts are barely warm; toast them separately.
- **Crowding or stacking them.** The ones not touching the pan steam rather than toast, and the
  batch comes out patchy.
- **Adding them early, or tossing them through the dressing.** They absorb moisture and go
  leathery, which wastes the whole exercise.
