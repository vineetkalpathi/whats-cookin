# Dry Roasting

Toasting seeds, dal or spices in a dry pan - no fat at all - until they smell nutty. [[tomato-soup]]
teaches it explicitly and says why: the moong dal is roasted "to get nice aroma" before any water
goes near it.

## Why it works
Two things happen in the pan at once. The surface of the seed dries out and then browns, which is
the Maillard reaction between its proteins and its own small amount of sugar - that is where the
nutty, roasted smell comes from, because none of it exists in a raw seed. At the same time the
volatile aromatic oils sitting in the seed coat are warmed to the point where they lift off and
you can smell them.

The absence of fat is what makes it a different technique from [[blooming-spices]] rather than a
variation on it:

- **No fat means nothing to conduct or buffer the heat.** The seed touches the pan metal
  directly, so its contact points run far hotter than they would in oil, and the whole thing
  happens faster and burns far more easily.
- **No fat means nothing to dissolve the aroma into.** In blooming, the flavour ends up in the
  oil, which then carries it through the dish. Here it stays in the seed, so the seed itself has
  to go into the food - roasted and then boiled, as in the soup, or scattered on top, as the
  roasted [[sesame-seeds]] are in [[methi-lagaan]].
- **Dry also means no water.** A wet seed cannot get above the boiling point of water at the
  surface, so it steams rather than toasts. This is why [[tomato-soup]] is emphatic that the dal
  is rinsed **after** roasting, not before: rinse first and you spend the pan's heat drying the
  dal, and it never browns.

## Steps
1. Heat a dry, heavy pan over medium. A [[skillet]] is fine. No oil, and no rinsing beforehand.
2. Add the seeds or dal in a single layer, so every piece is touching the pan.
3. Keep them moving almost constantly - shaking the pan or stirring. There is no fat to even out
  the heat, so anything that sits still scorches on one face.
4. Moong dal takes 2-3 minutes, per [[tomato-soup]]. Sesame seeds take less.
5. Tip them straight out of the pan when they are ready. A hot pan carries on roasting them after
  the heat is off.
6. Only now rinse, if the recipe rinses. The soup washes the dal at this point, then boils it in
  2 cups of water.

## When it's done
You smell it before you see it: a distinct nutty, popcorn-ish smell is the main cue. The dal goes
one shade darker, with the odd golden fleck. Sesame seeds start to jump in the pan and turn pale
gold. Dark brown or black specks, or any acrid smell, mean it has gone past roasted into bitter,
and the bitterness will carry into the finished dish.

## Common mistakes
- **Rinsing first.** Wet seeds steam. The pan's heat goes into evaporating the water and the
  surface never reaches browning temperature, so you get damp dal and no aroma.
- **Heat too high.** The outside scorches before the inside has dried, which tastes burnt and
  raw at the same time.
- **Walking away.** With no fat there is no margin; the gap between nutty and burnt is well under
  a minute.
- **A crowded or multi-layered pan.** The pieces not touching the metal only steam in the others'
  moisture, so the batch roasts unevenly.
- **Mixing sizes.** Whole seeds and split dal in the same pan means one is burnt by the time the
  other is ready.
- **Grinding or blending while hot.** If the roasted seeds are going into a blender, let them
  cool first; warm seeds release their oil and clag up instead of grinding.
